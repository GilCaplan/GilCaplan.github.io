The course (097400, Introduction to Causal Inference) covers the modern toolkit for recovering cause-and-effect from observational data: potential outcomes & counterfactuals, causal graphs (DAGs) and identification via backdoor adjustment, propensity-score matching & weighting (IPW), instrumental variables / 2SLS, regression discontinuity, and causal ML. The project applies it to one concrete question drawn from US Census data.

Treatment MIL — ever served Outcome PINCP > $50k Data ACS 2018 PUMS Source folktables

The causal question

Does serving in the US military causally increase a veteran's personal income compared to otherwise similar civilians?

  • Temporal ordering is structurally guaranteed — service is a completed past event; current income cannot have caused it.
  • Confounding is the whole challenge. Enlistment is non-random: people who serve disproportionately come from lower-income, rural backgrounds with lower pre-service education — all of which independently depress income — while service itself confers job training, federal hiring preference, and GI Bill benefits that raise it.

What the gap actually looks like

Computed directly from the committed acs_2018_slim.csv (adults 25–64, valid income, n = 1,657,499):

GroupnP(income ≥ $50k)
Served (MIL 1–3)108,0270.488
Never served (MIL 4)1,549,4720.352
Naive difference—+0.137 (+13.7 pp)

After standardizing over age × sex (a partial backdoor adjustment), the gap falls to +7.7 pp — nearly half the naive number was confounding by who serves, not the effect of serving. Adjusting further for the unobserved background confounders in the DAG (family SES, pre-service ability/health) would move it more. This is the project's core lesson, and it is exactly what the live demos let you reproduce.

The project DAG — drawn from scripts/causal_graph.py

Built exactly as the repo's graphviz script does: background confounders (some unobserved, dashed) feed into both treatment and outcome; post-treatment mediators sit on the causal path. The teal edge is the direct treatment effect we want to identify; the confounding backdoor paths are what adjustment has to block.

■ treatment / outcome & direct effect ■ observed confounders ■ unobserved confounders (dashed) ■ mediators (post-treatment)

Why the structure matters

Backdoor adjustment says: to identify MIL → PINCP, condition on the pre-treatment confounders (age, sex, race, place of birth, nativity, and ideally the unobserved SES/ability/health) — but not on the mediators (education attained, occupation, class of worker), since those lie on the causal path and conditioning on them would block part of the very effect we are estimating. The repo's DAG cleanly separates the two roles.

Live · Simpson's paradox in the military-income data

Each bar is a real age band from the ACS file with its real served/civilian income rates. The naive estimate pools everyone; the adjusted estimate is a backdoor adjustment — a weighted average of the within-band gaps (standardization). Slide the weights to see how a confounder that shifts who serves across bands inflates the naive number while the adjusted one stays honest.

Live · Propensity-score matching & the ATT

A 2-D synthetic-but-illustrative cohort: treated (served) vs control (civilian) units placed by two pre-treatment covariates (an age/SES axis). Treated units are selected toward the lower-left — the classic enlistment-selection pattern. Nearest-neighbour matching within a caliper pairs each treated unit to the closest control; the ATT is averaged over the matched pairs only. Tighten the caliper to trade bias for sample.

Tighter calipers cut bias (better covariate balance) but drop treated units that have no nearby control — the common-support trade-off. The true synthetic effect baked into the data is +0.10; the naive diff overshoots because of selection, and matching pulls the estimate back toward truth.
