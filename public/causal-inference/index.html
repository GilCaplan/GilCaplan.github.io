Does Maternal Smoking During Pregnancy Cause Infant Death?

A causal-inference course project (with Jeremy Jornet) on the CDC's 2015 Cohort Linked Birth/Infant Death Data Set — 3,988,733 US births. Treatment = sustained daily smoking (≥1 cigarette/day, ≥1 trimester); outcome = death before age one. The naive gap is +0.52 pp — seven causal estimators agree the real effect is about +0.25 pp. The demos below let you take it apart.

The course (097400, Introduction to Causal Inference) covers the modern toolkit for recovering cause-and-effect from observational data. The final project applies it to a real public-health question on the CDC's linked birth/infant-death microdata — one of the largest cohorts ever used for this question in a formal causal-identification framework.

Treatment sustained smoking — ≥1 cig/day, ≥1 trimester Outcome death before age 1 Data 2015 CDC Linked Birth/Infant Death n 3,988,733 births

The causal question

Does daily cigarette smoking during at least one trimester of pregnancy causally increase the probability of infant death before the first birthday?

  • Temporal ordering is structurally guaranteed — smoking occurs before or during birth, while death can only occur afterward.
  • Confounding is the whole challenge. Mothers who smoke tend to already be in less favorable socioeconomic and health conditions than non-smoking mothers, independently of smoking itself — education, insurance status, marital status, and pre-pregnancy health all shift both who smokes and infant-mortality risk.

Across seven adjusted causal estimators (next tab), the effect comes out to ≈+0.25 pp (≈2.5 extra deaths per 1,000 births) — roughly half of the naive gap is confounding by who smokes, not the causal effect of smoking. That gap, and how confident we should be in what's left, is exactly what the live demos let you reproduce.

Unobserved confounders (not adjusted — sensitivity-tested)Maternal mental health / stress, alcohol & drug use

The dataset is rich, but none of the unobserved confounders are directly measured — this is flagged in the report as the single most consequential and fragile assumption of the analysis.

Live · How much hidden confounding would erase the effect?

Each estimator has a tipping point λ* — the smallest hidden-confounding strength (in residual-SD units) at which its 95% CI would start to include zero. Drag the slider to test an assumed bias strength against all seven.

Assumed hidden-confounding λ 0.000 SD

All seven tip at small, similar values (λ* between 0.0189 and 0.0264) — a required bias of only 0.155–0.220 pp, about 30–43% of the baseline non-smoker mortality risk (0.5162%). That magnitude is plausible for unobserved confounders like maternal stress, chronic distress, or alcohol/drug use — which is why confidence in the result is moderate, not high.

Sensitivity to the treatment definition (smoking intensity)

The primary analysis treats any sustained smoking as equally exposed. Redefining treatment by the highest smoking-intensity category reported in any trimester gives:

Highest intensity1–5/day6–10/day11–20/day21–40/day41+/day
Treated mothers (n)100,907118,36674,1447,0471,584
Treated share of sample2.69%3.14%1.99%0.19%0.04%
Naive risk difference (pp)0.4670.4940.4860.6470.999

For the three common categories (1–5, 6–10, 11–20/day) estimates stay stable and positive, with most estimators showing a modest increasing dose-response trend (PSM is the exception). From 21–40/day onward results become unstable — several CIs include zero, and at 41+/day the trimmed IPW and AIPW estimates turn negative. That category is 0.19% and 0.04% of the sample respectively, too rare to resolve reliably. The report also flags that the PSM confidence interval for the 6–10/day category is internally inconsistent with its point estimate and should be treated with caution.

Sensitivity to treatment imbalance (undersampling controls)

Only 8.5% of mothers are treated. Re-running the full analysis under random and propensity-score-guided undersampling of controls (ratios down to 1:1), most estimates and CIs stay close to the full-sample results. Two exceptions: IPW (no trimming) gives consistently larger estimates under propensity-guided undersampling, and the S-learner is more sensitive to random undersampling at a 1:1 ratio. The T-learner — which fits separate outcome models per treatment group — is the most stable estimator across every undersampling scheme, consistent with it being least affected by the treatment imbalance in the first place.

Limitations (from the report)

  • No direct measures of maternal mental health, stress, alcohol or drug use — exactly the tipping-point risk.
  • Bootstrap uncertainty from only B=10 replications (refitting all models on ~4M rows per replication is expensive).
  • The two heaviest smoking-intensity categories stay inconclusive — too few treated mothers.
  • A single 2015 US birth cohort; generalization to other years or countries is untested.

All seven share the same four identification assumptions and rely on the same MLP propensity model (for IPW/PSM/AIPW) — agreement across genuinely different statistical models is itself evidence of robustness, independent of any single model being exactly right.

This project's contribution: rather than one adjusted-association model, it combines seven causal estimators under an explicit identification strategy with an extensive sensitivity-analysis suite — quantifying not just the effect, but how much hidden confounding it would take to explain it away.
